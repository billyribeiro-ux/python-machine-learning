Backtesting & QuantStats Tearsheets

A returns series isn't a result until you've measured it honestly. We define the metrics that matter (and compute them from scratch), turn them into a professional QuantStats tearsheet, and confront the two things that invalidate most backtests: overfitting and costs.

1. The metrics, defined precisely

We never use a number we can't compute ourselves, so quantlab/backtest/stats.py implements the core set. Know what each one answers:

MetricAnswers
CAGRWhat did I actually compound at, per year?
SharpeReturn per unit of total volatility (annualized)
SortinoReturn per unit of downside volatility
Max drawdownWorst peak-to-trough loss (the "could I stick with it?" number)
CalmarCAGR ÷ |max drawdown| — return per unit of worst pain
Win rateFraction of active periods that were positive
🔬 Annualization is a choice, not a constant

Sharpe multiplies the per-period ratio by √(periods per year). Daily → √252; 5-minute → √(252·78). Hard-code 252 and your intraday Sharpe is silently wrong by ~9×. That's why our stats functions take a periods_per_year argument and quantlab.utils.annualization_factor centralizes the mapping. Correctness across timeframes is a design decision.

2. Runnable: stats + a full tearsheet

Our quantlab/backtest/tearsheet.py bridges to QuantStats, sanitizing the index (QuantStats predates tz-aware/non-nanosecond indexes) and writing a shareable HTML report with rolling Sharpe, underwater drawdown plots, and monthly heatmaps — benchmarked against buy & hold.

3. Costs and slippage: where paper edges die

Every position change pays the spread, commissions, and market impact. Our engine charges costs on turnover; vectorbt takes fees and slippage. A strategy that trades often can look brilliant gross and lose money net. Always re-run with realistic costs and watch how Sharpe degrades — a robust edge survives; an overfit one evaporates.

4. Walk-forward: the only honest estimate

A single in-sample backtest tells you how well you fit the past. To estimate the future, walk forward: optimize on a window, test on the next untouched window, roll, repeat. The concatenated out-of-sample returns are your honest track record.

⚠️ The deflated Sharpe

If you tried N strategy variants and report the best Sharpe, the expected maximum of N noisy estimates is high even with zero real edge. The "deflated Sharpe ratio" adjusts for how many trials you ran. Practically: track how many configurations you tested, prefer robust parameter regions over single peaks, and always validate out-of-sample. A backtest is a hypothesis, not a promise.

🛠️ Try it

Run code/m10_quantstats.py and open the generated tearsheet.html. Find the worst drawdown on the underwater plot and the longest losing month on the monthly heatmap — then ask yourself honestly whether you'd have held through them. That gut-check is what separates a backtest from a strategy you can actually trade.
