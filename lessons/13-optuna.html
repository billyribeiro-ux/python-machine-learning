Optuna: Optimize Everything

One optimizer for both worlds: tune indicator/strategy settings and ML hyperparameters. We optimize what actually matters — backtested performance — with smart sampling, pruning, and multi-objective trade-offs, while guarding against the overfitting that optimization makes dangerously easy.

1. The study / trial model

Optuna's abstraction is simple and powerful: you write an objective(trial) that suggests parameter values and returns a score; Optuna runs many trials, learning where good regions are. Because the objective is just Python, it can build a strategy, backtest it, and return its Sharpe — so you optimize real performance, not a proxy like validation accuracy.

3. Samplers & pruning

The default TPE sampler models the distribution of good vs bad parameters and focuses sampling on promising regions — much faster than grid or random search for the same quality. Pruning abandons hopeless trials early (we use TrialPruned to reject invalid fast≥slow combos instantly; for ML you'd prune trials whose intermediate validation score is poor). Together they let you search a large space cheaply.

4. Multi-objective: there is no single "best"

Return and drawdown trade off. Maximizing only Sharpe might pick a strategy you couldn't stomach. Multi-objective optimization returns a Pareto front: the set of configurations where you can't improve one objective without worsening the other. Picking from the front is a judgement call about your risk tolerance — exactly right, because optimization should inform decisions, not pretend to make them.

🔬 Optimize ML hyperparameters too

The same machinery tunes model hyperparameters. An objective can suggest XGBoost's max_depth, learning_rate, and subsample, train with our time-ordered split, and return the backtested Sharpe of the resulting signal — so the model is optimized for trading PnL, not log-loss. One optimizer, both the strategy layer and the model layer.

6. Capstone: CPCV & the Probability of Backtest Overfitting

A hold-out is one cut of history; a clever search can still get lucky on it. The gold standard (Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu) asks a deeper question across many symmetric recombinations of train and test: how often is the in-sample best config no better than the median out of sample? That fraction is the Probability of Backtest Overfitting (PBO), computed by Combinatorially Symmetric Cross-Validation (CSCV).

🔬 How to read PBO (validated on known truth)

We tested the estimator against ground truth: a basket of pure-noise configs yields PBO ≈ 0.5 (selection is luck), while a genuinely dominant config yields PBO ≈ 0.0 (the winner stays best out of sample). So: PBO near 0 = the search is finding real edge; PBO near 0.5+ = your "best" strategy is probably a curve-fit. Pair it with the performance degradation slope (OOS regressed on IS — you want it positive and near 1, not negative).

Its companion, Combinatorial Purged CV (CombinatorialPurgedCV), tests on every combination of k groups at once to reconstruct φ = k·C(N,k)/N distinct backtest paths — turning a single Sharpe into a distribution. Judge a strategy by its bad paths, not its average:

7. The capstone: a one-call go/no-go dossier

Every check in this module — honest optimization, Deflated Sharpe, PBO, the CPCV fan, cost sensitivity — is wired into a single function, quantlab.research.strategy_report, that emits a GO / CONDITIONAL / NO-GO verdict with the reasons. The decision logic (evaluate_gate) is a pure function of the headline numbers, so it's deterministic and unit-tested separately from the (stochastic) search.

The point estimate looked fine — hold-out Sharpe 0.57, Deflated Sharpe 0.64 — yet the dossier says NO-GO because PBO across the search space is 70%: the strategy family overfits, so the single best config can't be trusted. ⚠️ Optimization is overfitting with extra steps

The more trials you run, the more the "best" result is fitted to noise in your backtest window. Three defenses: (1) optimize on a training period and confirm on a held-out one (walk-forward, Module 10); (2) prefer broad, stable regions of the Pareto front over razor-thin peaks; (3) keep the parameter space small and meaningful. Persist the study (storage="sqlite:///study.db") so you can audit how many trials you really ran — that count feeds the deflated-Sharpe reality check.

5. The honest optimizer: hold-out + Deflated Sharpe

We packaged the entire discipline into one function, quantlab.research.optimize_strategy, so you never have to remember to do it by hand. It optimizes any research.templates strategy and returns a result you can trust or distrust on sight:

  • Hold-out discipline — the series is split chronologically; the optimizer only ever sees the train portion, and the reported edge is the untouched hold-out.
  • Robust objective — it maximizes the mean minus the spread of per-fold Sharpes, rewarding strategies that work across regimes rather than one lucky stretch.
  • Deflated Sharpe Ratio — given how many trials you ran, how likely is this hold-out Sharpe to be more than luck? This is the number that survives review.

A real run flags itself as overfit — which is the point

train Sharpe: 0.67 HOLD-OUT Sharpe (honest): -0.20 IS -> OOS gap: 0.87 Deflated Sharpe (60 trials): 0.087 verdict: LIKELY OVERFIT
🧱 A framework that refuses to lie to you

A naive search would have reported "Sharpe 0.67!" and moved on. Ours shows the hold-out Sharpe is actually negative and the Deflated Sharpe is 0.09 — i.e. this result is exactly what luck produces across 60 trials. The most valuable thing a research tool can do is tell you when not to trade. This is wired into the Streamlit Optimize page (Module 16) with a live trustworthy/over-fit verdict, the hold-out equity curve, and the Pareto front.

🛠️ Try it

Run code/honest_research.py, then raise n_trials from 60 to 400 and watch the Deflated Sharpe fall even if the raw best Sharpe rises — more searching makes the same result less believable. Then widen the hold-out to 0.4 and see how the in-sample→out-of-sample gap behaves. Internalizing this relationship is what separates a quant from a curve-fitter.
