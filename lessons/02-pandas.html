pandas for Market Data

NumPy gives you speed; pandas gives you a labeled, time-aware home for that speed. Master the DatetimeIndex, resampling, rolling/ewm, groupby, and multi-asset alignment — plus the three pitfalls (chained indexing, copy-vs-view, and look-ahead) that quietly corrupt results — and you can wrangle any market dataset with confidence.

1. The index is the whole game

In ordinary data work the row index is an afterthought. In time-series finance it is the feature: it carries time, timezone, and frequency, and pandas uses it to align data automatically. Two series with different dates add together by matching timestamps, not positions — which is exactly what you want across assets with different trading calendars, and exactly what bites you if you don't expect it.

🔬 Timezones are a correctness issue, not a nicety

A "9:30 AM" bar means nothing without a timezone. Mixing tz-naive and tz-aware timestamps raises errors in modern pandas (good — it's refusing to guess). Our data layer normalizes everything to UTC so math is unambiguous; convert to exchange-local time only for display with df.tz_convert("America/New_York"). Store in UTC, present in local — the same rule every serious system uses.

2. Resampling: change the timeframe correctly

Aggregating bars (1-minute → 5-minute, daily → weekly) is not averaging — OHLCV has rules: open is the first, high is the max, low is the min, close is the last, volume is the sum. Get this wrong and every downstream indicator is subtly off. pandas' resample with a per-column aggregation does it correctly.

⚠️ Don't .mean() your OHLC

A surprising amount of code resamples with a single .mean() or .last() for all columns. That destroys the high/low (you lose the actual range) and fabricates an "open" that never traded. Always aggregate column-by-column. The same OHLCV_AGG map works for any timeframe pair.

3. Rolling, expanding, and EWM

These three are the heart of indicator construction in pandas. Rolling = fixed look-back window. Expanding = everything up to now (anchored). EWM = exponential decay (recent bars weighted more). All three are window-aware and, critically, all three only look backward — they never peek ahead.

4. groupby: per-symbol operations done right

The tidy/long panel from get_ohlcv_multi stacks many symbols in one frame. To compute an indicator per symbol you must group by symbol first — otherwise a rolling mean bleeds AAPL's prices into MSFT's. groupby(...).transform() keeps the original shape, which is what you want for adding feature columns.

⚠️ The #1 multi-asset bug

Running a rolling/shift/pct_change on a stacked panel without grouping leaks one symbol's data into the next at the seams. The result looks fine (right shape, plausible numbers) and is wrong. Rule: on a long panel, every time-series op is preceded by groupby("symbol").

Trap B — copy vs view

Slicing sometimes returns a view (shares memory) and sometimes a copy. If you intend to modify a subset as its own object, call .copy() explicitly. Ambiguity here is the source of most "why didn't my change stick?" bugs.

Trap C — look-ahead via shift (the dangerous one)

🧱 Make the safe path the easy path

We never re-derive the shift by hand in strategy code. Backward-safe signals use .shift(1) at the one place signals become positions; ML labels use quantlab.utils.forward_returns, which builds the future-looking target and leaves NaNs at the tail so you literally cannot train on rows whose outcome isn't known yet. The best way to avoid a bug class is to encapsulate the correct version.
