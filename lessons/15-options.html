Module 15 · Putting It Together

Options: Chains, IV & Greeks

Options add a second dimension — volatility — to trading. We pull chains through the same provider interface, then build Black-Scholes pricing, the Greeks, and implied volatility from scratch in vectorized NumPy, so you understand and trust every number on the surface.

1. Options data through the provider

We already implemented get_options_chain on YahooProvider: it returns a single tidy frame of calls and puts, each row tagged with option_type and expiry. Same philosophy as OHLCV — a normalized shape you can filter, scan, and compute on, regardless of vendor.

2. Black-Scholes from first principles

The Black-Scholes price is the discounted, risk-neutral expected payoff of a European option. Our quantlab/options/black_scholes.py implements it vectorized — pass scalars or whole arrays (an entire chain at once). The two intermediate quantities d1 and d2 drive both the price and every Greek.

3. The Greeks — your risk dashboard

The Greeks are the option's sensitivities, and trading options is trading Greeks. Delta (spot), gamma (delta's convexity), vega (volatility), theta (time decay), rho (rates). Ours returns all five at once, vectorized over a chain.

4. Implied volatility: invert the model

The market quotes a price; IV is the volatility that makes Black-Scholes reproduce it. There's no closed form, so we solve numerically. Our implementation uses bisection — slower than Newton-Raphson but rock-solid: it can't diverge, and it handles the deep ITM/OTM cases where vega→0 breaks Newton. For a scanner over thousands of contracts, robustness beats raw speed.

Expected output (numbers vary)

call=7.517 recovered IV=0.2500 (input 0.25) strike bid ask impliedvolatility iv_ours 738.0 2.41 2.45 0.1000 0.1695 ...
⚠️ Your IV won't exactly match the vendor's

Expect divergence, especially for near-expiry contracts (tiny T makes IV hyper-sensitive) and because vendors use different rate/dividend assumptions, American-style early-exercise models, and last-trade vs mid prices. The round-trip test (0.25 → 0.2500) proves our math is correct; the chain difference is a modeling-assumption gap, not a bug. Knowing why they differ is the mark of understanding the model rather than calling a black box.

5. From Greeks to strategies and scans

With pricing, Greeks, and IV in hand you can evaluate real structures — covered calls, vertical spreads — by their payoff and risk profile, and build options scanners: rank underlyings by IV rank (current IV vs its 1-year range) to find rich premium for selling, or cheap convexity for buying; screen for delta-targeted strikes; flag unusual volume. The same scan-and-rank pattern from Module 14, applied to the chain.

🛠️ Try it

Build a covered-call screen: for each name in a universe, pull the chain, find the ~30-delta call ~30 days out (use our greeks to locate the strike whose delta ≈ 0.30), and rank by annualized premium yield (call mid ÷ spot, scaled to a year). Add an IV-rank filter to prefer selling when volatility is historically high. You've built an income-strategy scanner on your own pricing engine.
